The problem

The burden repeats every time the site needs care

Maintaining a catheter site often means layering separate securement, coverage, antimicrobial, and preparation products. Each scheduled—or unscheduled—change adds sterile steps, nursing time, and another round of adhesive removal. These are recurring themes in our clinician discovery conversations.

Where the current workflow creates friction

A description of common practice, not a claim about any specific product.

  • Securement and site coverage are handled by separate products and separate application steps.
  • A dressing that lifts, becomes soiled, or gets wet can trigger an unscheduled change.
  • Inspecting and cleansing the site generally requires removing and replacing adhesive coverage.
  • Maintenance creates another opportunity to disturb the catheter or the surrounding skin.
  • Keeping the site dry during bathing is managed as a separate task.

How it affects clinicians and patients

Themes raised in clinician discovery about workflow and patient experience.

  • Pain, irritation, and skin trauma associated with repeated adhesive removal.
  • Extra nursing time for a recurring, multi-step task.
  • Clinic visits or home-health coordination built around routine site care.
  • Complexity for patients and caregivers trying to manage life with a long-term line.
  • Continuity challenges as patients move across inpatient, outpatient, and home settings.

Actual products, protocols, and time vary by institution. We use each site’s own workflow during customer discovery.

The device

Meet SnapCath™

SnapCath is an external accessory being developed for indwelling vascular access catheters. It is designed to be applied over the insertion site after the line is placed and to combine two jobs: holding the catheter in position and covering the site.

It is a single unit with three parts:

  • An adhesive base designed to attach to the skin around the insertion site and remain in place for up to 28 days.
  • A rigid shell that holds the catheter and sits over the site.
  • A hinged, replaceable lid designed to open for routine site access and be changed on the weekly maintenance schedule.

Why that split matters: in the planned 28-day workflow, one base placement would be followed by three routine lid changes. The design goal is to let the clinician open, cleanse, assess, and replace the lid without peeling the base from the skin each week.

Current development focus: the first configuration is being designed around 3–5 Fr PICC lines for patients whose access remains in place for days or weeks. Customer discovery includes inpatient vascular access, outpatient and home infusion, dialysis, long-term care, and health-system teams.

Proposed workflow

Designed to simplify routine site care

The change is not only the number of products. It is the sequence clinicians and patients repeat throughout the life of the line.

Common multi-product workflow

Protocols vary by institution.

  • Apply securement, site coverage, and other protocol-required products in separate steps.
  • Remove the adhesive dressing to assess and cleanse the site.
  • Rebuild the bundle after each scheduled or unscheduled change.
  • Repeat training and stocking decisions across each product in the bundle.

SnapCath design objective

A development concept, not tested performance.

  • Use one device to anchor the catheter and cover the site.
  • Open the hinged lid for routine assessment and cleansing.
  • Replace the lid while leaving the adhesive base in place.
  • Use a consistent core workflow across inpatient, outpatient, and home settings.

SnapCath does not currently include an integrated antimicrobial element. We want to understand how each institution’s existing infection-prevention protocol would interact with the proposed workflow.

Who from my institution should be involved?

The answer depends on your setting. It may include vascular access, infusion or dialysis leadership, nursing operations, infection prevention, value analysis, product review, supply chain, or a home-infusion clinical leader. A name or introduction is helpful, but not required.

Which lines and care settings are the current focus?

The first configuration is being designed around 3–5 Fr PICC lines for patients whose access remains in place for days or weeks. Customer discovery includes inpatient vascular access, outpatient and home infusion, dialysis, long-term care, and health-system teams. Broader line applications are not part of the current first-configuration focus.

How does the current design address site access and CHG?

The hinged lid is designed to open for routine site assessment and cleansing while the adhesive base remains in place. The current SnapCath concept does not include an integrated CHG element and should not be presented as an infection-prevention outcome. Understanding how the design could fit each institution’s existing protocol is part of customer discovery.

The most useful inputs are your current product bundle, contract pricing, scheduled and unscheduled change frequency, staff time, patient volume, line dwell time, and care setting. We use those inputs to build a site-specific discussion for clinical and product-review stakeholders.

Could a discovery conversation lead to a pilot or letter of intent?

Yes, if the clinical need, proposed workflow, and institutional priorities align. Any pilot planning or letter of intent would be a later, separate step with an authorized institutional representative. The initial clinician inquiry is simply the start of that process.

SnapCath is still in development. There is no product available for purchase and no evaluation timeline being promised on this page. Institutions participating in discovery can stay close to the program as future evaluation plans take shape.
